Exercises

  1. (a) If $(\Omega, {\cal F}, \mathrm{P})$ is a probability space and $A, B\in {\cal F}$ are events such that $\mathrm{P}(B)\neq 0$, the $\textbf{conditional probability of } A \textbf{ given } B$ is denoted $\mathrm{P}(A | B)$ and defined by

    $$ \mathrm{P}(A | B) = \frac{\mathrm{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathrm{P}(B)}. $$

    Prove the $\textbf{total probability formula:}$ if $A,B_1,B_2,\ldots,B_k \in {\cal F}$ such that $\Omega$ is the disjoint union of $B_1,\ldots, B_k$ and $\mathrm{P}(B_i) \neq 0$ for $1\le i\le k$, then

    $$ \begin{equation} \tag{21} \mathrm{P}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^k \mathrm{P}(B_i) \mathrm{P}(A | B_i). \end{equation} $$

    (b) An urn initially contains one white ball and one black ball. At each step of the experiment, a ball is drawn at random from the urn, then put back and another ball of the same color is added. Prove that the number of white balls that are in the urn after $N$ steps is a uniform random number in $\{1,2,\ldots,N+1\}$. That is, the event that the number of white balls after step $N$ is equal to $k$ has probability $1/(N+1)$ for each $1\le k\le N+1$. (Note: The idea is to use (21), but there is no need to be too formal about constructing the relevant probability space --- you can assume an intuitive notion of probabilities.)

  3. Let $(\Omega, {\cal F})$ be a measurable space. A $\textbf{pre-probability measure}$ is a function $\mathrm{P}:{\cal F}\to [0,1]$ that satisfies

    $$ \begin{equation} \tag{22} \mathrm{P}(\emptyset) = 0, \qquad \mathrm{P}(\Omega) = 1. \end{equation} $$

    If a pre-probability measure $\mathrm{P}$ satisfies

    $$ \begin{equation} \tag{23} A_1, A_2, \ldots \in {\cal F}\textrm{ are pairwise disjoint} \implies \mathrm{P}(\cup_{n=1}^\infty A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathrm{P}(A_n). \end{equation} $$

    then we say that it is $\sigma\textbf{-additive}$. If it satisfies

    $$ \begin{equation} \tag{24} A_1,\ldots ,A_n \in {\cal F}\textrm{ are pairwise disjoint} \implies \mathrm{P}(\cup_{k=1}^n A_k) = \sum_{k=1}^n \mathrm{P}(A_k). \end{equation} $$

    then we say that it is $\textbf{additive}$. Recall that we defined a $\textbf{probability measure}$ to be a pre-probability measure that is $\sigma$-additive.

    We say that a pre-probability measure satisfies the $\textbf{continuity}$ properties if it satisfies

    $$ \begin{equation} \tag{25} (A_n)_{n=1}^\infty \subset {\cal F}, \ \ A_n \subset A_{n+1}\ \forall n \implies \mathrm{P}(\cup_{n=1}^\infty A_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \mathrm{P}(A_n), \end{equation} $$
    $$ \begin{equation} \tag{26} (A_n)_{n=1}^\infty \subset {\cal F}, \ \ A_n \supset A_{n+1}\ \forall n \implies \mathrm{P}(\cap_{n=1}^\infty A_n) = \lim_{n\to\infty} \mathrm{P}(A_n). \end{equation} $$

    Prove that a probability measure satisfies the continuity properties, and that an additive pre-probability measure that satisfies the first continuity property (25) ("continuity from below") is $\sigma$-additive, and is therefore a probability measure.

  4. (a) A coin has some bias $p\in (0,1)$, so when tossed it comes up Heads with probability $p$, or Tails with probability $1-p$. Suppose the coin is tossed $N$ times independently, and let $A_{N,k}$ denote the event that the result came up Heads exactly $k$ times.     is true. You may submit a short written explanation to test your understanding, but it is not required.

    (b) A group of $N$ prisoners is locked up in a prison, each in a separate cell with no ability to communicate with the other prisoners. Each cell contains a mysterious on/off electrical switch. One evening the warden visits each of the prisoners and presents them with the following dilemma: During the night each prisoner must choose whether to leave his switch in the on or off position. If at midnight exactly one of the switches is in the on position, all the prisoners will be set free in the morning; otherwise they will all be executed!

    The prisoners cannot coordinate their actions, but they are all rational, know calculus and probability theory, and each is equipped with a random number generator. For extra fun, try to guess in advance how big or small you expect the survival likelihood to be, and see how your guess measures up to the actual result.

    2. $\sigma({\cal A})$ is the minimal $\sigma$-algebra satisfying property 1 above, in the sense that if ${\cal F}$ is any other $\sigma$-algebra that contains all the elements of ${\cal A}$, then $\sigma({\cal A}) \subset {\cal F}$.

  7. (a) Prove the $\textbf{inclusion-exclusion principle}$: If $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ are events in a probability space $(\Omega, {\cal F}, \mathrm{P})$, then

  15. If $P,Q$ are two probability measures on a measurable space $(\Omega, {\cal F})$, we say that $\textbf{$P$is absolutely continuous with respect to } Q$, and denote this $P<<Q$, if for any $A\in {\cal F}$, if $Q(A)=0$ then $P(A)=0$.

    Prove that $P<<Q$ if and only if for any $\epsilon>0$ there exists a $\delta>0$ such that if $A\in {\cal F}$ and $Q(A)<\delta$ then $P(A)<\epsilon$.

    $\textbf{Hint.}$ Apply a certain famous lemma.

    $\textbf{Note.}$ The intuitive meaning of the relation $P<<Q$ is as follows: suppose there is a probabilistic experiment, and we are told that one of the measures $P$ or $Q$ governs the statistical behavior of the outcome, but we don't know which one. (This is a situation that arises frequently in real-life applications of probability and statistics.) All we can do is perform the experiment, observe the result, and make a guess. If $P<<Q$, any event which is observable with positive probability according to $P$ also has positive $Q$-probability, so we can never rule out $Q$ as the correct measure, although we may get an event with $Q(A)>0$ and $P(A)=0$ that enables us to rule out $P$. If we also have the symmetric relation $Q<<P$, then we can't rule out either of the measures.

  16. A function $\varphi:(a,b)\to\R$ is called $\textbf{convex}$ if for any $x,y\in (a,b)$ and $\alpha\in [0,1]$ we have

    (b) Prove $\textbf{Jensen's inequality}$, which says that if $X$ is a random variable such that $\mathrm{P}(X\in (a,b))=1$ and $\varphi:(a,b)\to\R$ is convex, then

    $$ \varphi(\mathbf{E}X) \le \mathbf{E}(\varphi(X)). $$

    $\textbf{Hint.}$ Start by proving the following property of a convex function: If $\varphi$ is convex then at any point $x_0\in (a,b)$, $\varphi$ has a $\textbf{supporting line}$, that is, a linear function $y(x)=ax+b$ such that $y(x_0)=\varphi(x_0)$ and such that $\varphi(x)\ge y(x)$ for all $x\in(a,b)$ (to prove its existence, use the characterization of convexity from part (a) to show that the left-sided derivative of $\varphi$ at $x_0$ is less than or equal to the right-sided derivative at $x_0$; the supporting line is a line passing through the point $(x_0,\varphi(x_0))$ whose slope lies between these two numbers). Now take the supporting line function at $x_0=\mathbf{E}X$ and see what happens.

    (b) Deduce from this lemma, using results we learned in class, the following rate of convergence result for the Strong Law of Large Numbers in the case of a finite variance: If $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ is an i.i.d. sequence such that $\mathbf{E} X_1 = 0$, $\mathbf{V}(X_1)<\infty$, and $S_n=\sum_{k=1}^n X_k$, then for any $\epsilon>0$,

    $$ \frac{S_n}{n^{1/2+\epsilon}} \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\textrm{a.s.}} 0. $$

    $\textbf{Notes.}$ When $X_1$ is a "random sign", i.e., a random variable that takes the values $-1,+1$ with respective probabilities $1/2,1/2$, the sequence of cumulative sums $(S_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ is often called a $\textbf{(symmetric) random walk on } \mathbb{Z}$, since it represents the trajectory of a walker starting from 0 and taking a sequence of independent jumps in a random (positive or negative) direction. An interesting question concerns the rate at which the random walk can drift away from its starting point. By the SLLN, it follows that almost surely, $S_n = o(n)$, so the distance of the random walk from the origin almost surely has sub-linear growth. By the exercise above, the stronger result $S_n = o(n^{1/2+\epsilon})$ also holds for all $\epsilon$. This is close to optimal, since by the Central Limit Theorem which we will discuss soon, one cannot hope to show that $S_n = o(n^{1/2})$. In fact, the "true" rate of growth is given by the following famous theorem, whose proof is a (somewhat complicated) elaboration on the techniques we have discussed.

    $\textbf{Theorem (The Law of the Iterated Logarithm; A. Y. Khinchin, 1924).}$

    $$ \mathrm{P}\left(\limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n \log\log n}} = 1\right) = 1. $$

    Therefore, by symmetry, also

    $$ \mathrm{P}\left(\liminf_{n\to\infty} \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{2n \log\log n}} = -1\right) = 1. $$

    It follows in particular that, almost surely, the random walk will cross the origin infinitely many times.
