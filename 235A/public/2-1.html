2.1 Basic definitions

We now formalize the concepts introduced in the previous lecture. It turns out that it's easiest to deal with events as subsets of a large set called the $\textbf{probability space}$, instead of as abstract logical statements. The logical operations of negation, conjunction and disjunction are replaced by the set-theoretic operations of taking the complement, intersection or union, but the intuitive meaning attached to those operations is the same.

Definition 2.1

AlgebraIf $\Omega$ is a set, an $\textbf{algebra}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a collection ${\cal F}$ of subsets of $\Omega$ that satisfies the following axioms: \begin{equation} \emptyset \in {\cal F}, \tag{A1}\qquad\qquad\qquad \end{equation} \begin{equation} \ \ A \in {\cal F} \implies \Omega\setminus A \in {\cal F}, \tag{A2} \\ \end{equation} \begin{equation} A,B \in {\cal F} \implies A\cup B \in {\cal F}. \tag{A3} \end{equation} A word synonymous with algebra in this context is $\textbf{field}$.

Definition 2.2

$\sigma$-algebraA $\textbf{$\sigma$-algebra}$ (also called a $\textbf{$\sigma$-field}$) is an algebra ${\cal F}$ that satisfies the additional axiom

$$ \begin{equation} A_1, A_2, A_3, \ldots \in \mathcal{F} \implies \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \in \mathcal{F}. \tag{A4} \end{equation} $$

Example 2.3

If $\Omega$ is any set, then $\{ \emptyset, \Omega \}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra -- in fact it is the smallest possible $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$. Similarly, the power set ${\cal P}(\Omega)$ of all subsets of $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra, and is (obviously) the largest $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$.

Definition 2.4

Measurable spaceA $\textbf{measurable space}$ is a pair ($\Omega,{\cal F})$ where $\Omega$ is a set and ${\cal F}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of subsets of $\Omega$.

Definition 2.5

Probability measureGiven a measurable space $(\Omega, {\cal F})$, a $\textbf{probability measure}$ on $(\Omega, {\cal F})$ is a function $\mathrm{P}:{\cal F}\to[0,1]$ that satisfies the properties:

$$ \begin{equation} \mathrm{P}(\emptyset) = 0, \quad \mathrm{P}(\Omega) = 1, \tag{P1} \end{equation} $$
$$ \begin{equation} A_1, A_2, \ldots \in {\cal F}\textrm{ are pairwise disjoint} \implies \mathrm{P}(\cup_{n=1}^\infty A_n) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mathrm{P}(A_n). \tag{P2} \end{equation} $$

Definition 2.6

Probability spaceA $\textbf{probability space}$ is a triple $(\Omega, {\cal F}, \mathrm{P})$, where $(\Omega, {\cal F})$ is a measurable space, and $\mathrm{P}$ is a probability measure on $(\Omega, {\cal F})$.

Intuitively, we think of $\Omega$ as representing the set of possible $\textbf{outcomes}$ of a probabilistic experiment, and refer to it as the $\textbf{sample space}$. The $\sigma$-algebra ${\cal F}$ is the $\textbf{$\sigma$-algebra of events}$, namely those subsets of $\Omega$ which have a well-defined probability (as we shall see later, it is not always possible to assign well-defined probabilities to all sets of outcomes). And $\mathrm{P}$ is the "notion" or "measure" of probability on our sample space.

Probability theory can be described loosely as the study of probability spaces (this is of course a gross oversimplification...). A more general mathematical theory called $\textbf{measure theory}$ studies $\textbf{measure spaces}$, which are like probability spaces except that the measures can take values in $[0,\infty]$ instead of $[0,1]$, and the total measure of the space is not necessarily equal to $1$ (such measures are referred to as $\textbf{$\sigma$-additive nonnegative measures}$). Measure theory is an important and non-trivial theory, and studying it requires a separate concentrated effort. We shall content ourselves with citing and using some of its most basic results. For proofs and more details, refer to Chapter 1 and the measure theory appendix in [Dur2010] or to a measure theory textbook.
